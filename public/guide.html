THE PURDUE FOUNDER FIELD GUIDE · 2026

Start somewhere.
Build something.

From first conversation to first customer. Read in order, or jump to where you are.
THE POINT

You do not need a startup idea, a pitch deck, or an investor to begin. You need a problem, people who feel it, and a small test. This guide tells you the next move at each stage and which Purdue doors are useful then.

01EXPLORE

NO IDEA REQUIRED

Find the room where builders are.

Show up once before you optimize anything. Listen to what people are making. Offer help. Ask what keeps breaking for them or their customers. Your goal is one real conversation and one return visit.

CURIOUS→GO TO ONE ROOM→MEET TWO BUILDERS
Do this this week
  1. Pick one Nightshift or Anvil event.
  2. Ask two builders, “What are you working on, and what is hard about it?”
  3. Write down one problem you noticed.

02VALIDATE

A PROBLEM, NOT A PITCH

Talk to the people who have it.

Before building, learn how people solve this today. A useful interview starts with a recent real example. “Tell me about the last time this happened” beats “Would you use my app?” Record the exact workaround and cost.

ASK THIS
“When did this last happen? What did you do? What did it cost you?”
Leave with evidence
  1. Speak to five people from the same customer group.
  2. Write the repeated pain and current workaround in one sentence.
  3. Take that evidence to Firestarter, Moonshot, or Venture X.

03BUILD

THE SMALLEST TEST

Make a version that can fail fast.

A spreadsheet, manual service, landing page, or simple prototype can test demand. Pick the cheapest form that lets a real user act. If the idea comes from Purdue research, talk to the Office of Technology Commercialization before sharing protected details publicly.

PROBLEM+SMALL TEST→REAL SIGNAL
Choose a build path
  1. Software or service: show a working prototype to one user.
  2. Hardware or science: ask HIVE where to test and find collaborators.
  3. University research: start with OTC disclosure.

04LAUNCH

GET THE FIRST YES

Put it in a real user's hands.

A launch is useful when somebody outside your team changes behavior. Aim for a pilot, repeat use, a payment, or a signed commitment. Share what happened with other founders and use feedback to improve the next version.

NOT YET SIGNAL“People thought it was cool.”
REAL SIGNAL“Three teams used it twice.”
After a first test
  1. Write down who used it, what they did, and what you learned.
  2. Try New Venture Challenge or BoilerLaunch when your team and evidence fit.
  3. Use buildpurdue Cohort for recurring founder accountability.

05FUND

CHOOSE FIT, THEN APPLY

Money is a tool. Pick the right kind.

Revenue, competitions, grants, campus funds, angels, and venture capital solve different problems. Start with the amount and milestone you need. Read each program's current eligibility and application window before investing time.

NEED TO LEARNUse customers, programs, or small competitions.
RESEARCH RISKConsider grants and non-dilutive funding.
FAST SCALINGExplore venture funds or accelerators.
Application shortlist
  1. Purdue: Purdue Ventures and Trask Innovation Fund.
  2. National: Y Combinator, a16z speedrun, and PearX.
  3. Research: NSF SBIR/STTR.

06SCALE

MAKE THE GOOD THING REPEAT

Build a company, not a demo.

When users come back, learn why. Make onboarding repeatable, clarify what one customer is worth, and hire for the bottleneck you can name. Stay close to the founder community while the team changes shape.

Three useful checks
  1. Can a new customer get value without founder hand-holding?
  2. Does each customer teach you how to reach the next one?
  3. Do you know the one constraint the next hire or dollar removes?
